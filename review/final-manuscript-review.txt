Final review of The weight-three Lambert sum at the golden ratio is irrational
Seven-page manuscript of 18 November 2026

Mathematical correctness

I read the manuscript from the definition of the Lambert sum through
the field norm. I found no gap in either theorem. In particular the
golden-ratio sum lies outside Q(sqrt(5)), and the value at 1+i lies
outside Q(i). The general theorem covers Salem bases without any
restriction on the arguments of their conjugates on the unit circle.

In Lemma 2.1 I checked the inherited local cyclotomic bounds, the
extracted base power and the degree sum against the integer-base
manuscript. The two degree contributions add to 321/(64 pi^2).
Integrality at every positive prime eliminates any remaining negative
power of the formal base. This step makes it legitimate to use the
same integer polynomial at all embeddings.

In Proposition 3.1 the triple zeros kill the first two Euler
derivatives at each completed node. Every differentiated term at the
remaining nodes retains the summable geometric factor. This gives
the stated positive cubic margin for complex bases as well as real ones.
In Lemma 4.1 the circle of radius 1+1/K separates the moduli of the
nodes. The moment recurrence includes the polynomial part of the partial
fractions. The maximum modulus principle then gives the claimed
O_Y(K^2 log^3(2K)) bound on the closed disk for each fixed Y.

In Proposition 5.1 the Cauchy transform has a genuine pole at each
inverse power of the base. If all sufficiently large determinants
vanished, the resulting rational interpolants would agree. The order
of the fixed error at infinity would bound the degrees of their common
factors, while interpolation requires those factors to vanish at an
unbounded number of distinct nodes. This proves nonvanishing on the
subsequence needed in Section 6. On that subsequence the field norm
is a positive integer in absolute value and the estimates force it
to tend to zero.

Exposition and sources

For a reader familiar with field norms and complex analysis, the
opening example gives a reason to introduce the formal value Y. At
the conjugate of the golden ratio the Lambert series diverges, while
the polynomial still has a value. The example with six linear
factors in Section 3 explains the cubic rate. The abstract states the
field exclusion with its full hypothesis.

One citation in Lemma 2.1 can be made more precise. The local estimate
for d at most K/2 is equation (4.8) of Section 4.2 of the integer-base
manuscript. Section 4.4 assembles the local estimates, and Proposition
A.1 supplies the improvement for d greater than K/2. An optional
addition to the introduction would pair the two examples at weight
one. On page 232 of his Kyoto paper, Tachiya's Theorem 1 covers 1+i
with constant periodic coefficients, and Theorem 2 covers the golden
ratio. The existing attribution for the golden ratio is accurate.
