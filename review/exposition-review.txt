The weight-three Lambert sum at the golden ratio is irrational
Author's exposition review of the seven-page manuscript, 18 November 2026

I read the introduction first with the abstract covered, then read the
proof in order. The intended reader knows basic algebraic number theory
and complex analysis and can take the cited cyclotomic estimates as input
through Lemma 2.1. I would keep the golden-ratio example first and the
field norm last.

The opening, page 1

Every partial sum at the golden ratio belongs to Q(sqrt(5)). That fact
gives the reader a concrete question about the limit before Theorem 1.1.
Under the field-membership hypothesis the limit would have a conjugate Y,
while the terms of the series at the conjugate base are asymptotic to
-n^2. The revised paragraph writes out the integer made from the two
polynomial values. It also explains why a nonreal expanding base can be
accompanied by its complex conjugate in Theorem 1.2. The abstract already
states the full hypothesis and the example 1+i.

The determinant and its size, pages 2–5

The Euler derivative appears with its purpose. Applying it twice gives
the numerator j^2, and summing over the geometric nodes gives the
denominator b^j-1. The text distinguishes the argument x of the functional
from the formal base t and the formal Lambert value X.

Before (2.2), the motivation for changing both polynomial bases now
appears on the same page as the determinant. In Section 3 the degree-six
example displays three zeros at each of the first two nodes. Its exponent
is 3·1+3·2. The general row exponent grows quadratically, and adding the
row and column exponents gives the cubic decay. The proof also identifies
the extra decay in the node index needed to sum the differentiated terms.

Section 4 explains both reasons for choosing the radius 1+1/K at the
point where it is chosen. The relative gaps between the poles are at least
1/(K+1), and the contribution (deg C_K) log(1+1/K) is only O(K^2).
The example P_1(1,Y)=-2 illustrates why the maximum modulus principle
is applied to the cleared polynomial.

Nonvanishing, pages 5–6

This was the passage that asked the reader to supply the most. The
revised proof constructs the error first, using D_k(z) to impose zeros at
the exterior nodes. It then obtains the numerator T_k by subtraction.
When the fraction is reduced, a common factor can account for an
interpolation zero on its own. The leading term of the fixed error at
infinity bounds the degree of that factor. This explains why it cannot
account for the growing number of prescribed zeros.
